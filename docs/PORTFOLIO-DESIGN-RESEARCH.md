# Portfolio design research and proposed direction

Prepared 4 November 2026 for Nikhil Yarra. This report addresses both visual quality and the practical task of evaluating the owner’s work. It does not claim that any single layout is scientifically established as the best portfolio.

## Recommendation

Build a visually distinctive research-and-engineering portfolio with an immediate project overview, substantial artifact previews and directly addressable case studies. Keep navigation predictable and text readable. The current draft is a functional baseline, but its repeated text/figure rows, broad pale surfaces and slogan-led exhibits do not yet provide enough individuality or depth. Passing overflow checks does not resolve that visual criticism.

The next composition should make the work more tangible: a real retrieval workspace, a genuinely inspectable regression comparison and a documentation repair whose evidence can be opened. Visual interest should come from these materials, typography and deliberate composition. The owner’s rejection of numbered section labels, arrows and cinematic backgrounds remains a firm design constraint.

## Research method and evidence strength

The review used peer-reviewed HCI studies hosted by their authors/institutions, NN/g’s original portfolio research and practitioner material, an original video transcript, a published live-review summary, and first-party portfolio examples. Findings about first impressions, hiring preferences and our own art direction are distinguished below.

No participants were recruited to test this portfolio. There is no measured hiring-conversion uplift, task-time improvement or universally optimal spacing/font/color result for Nikhil’s audience. Most hiring-specific material concerns UX hiring rather than AI engineering or European PhD admission; applying it here is an explicitly limited transfer.

## Evidence register

### Website complexity and familiarity

Tuch, Presslaber, Stoecklin, Opwis and Bargas-Avila (2012), *The role of visual complexity and prototypicality regarding first impression of websites*, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 70(11), 794–811, DOI 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2012.06.003.

The experiments used website screenshots and brief exposures. Lower complexity and familiar website structure received favorable aesthetic judgments in the tested conditions. This supports recognizable navigation and visual order. It does not establish that portfolios should be empty, monochrome or interchangeable, or that initial attractiveness predicts an interview.

Source: [Google Research publication record](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/).

### Visual appeal depends on more than “minimalism”

Reinecke et al. (2013), *Predicting Users’ First Impressions of Website Aesthetics with a Quantification of Perceived Visual Complexity and Colorfulness*, CHI, DOI 10.1145/2470654.2481281.

The authors collected ratings involving 450 website screenshots and 548 volunteers. Their models combined visual characteristics and demographic variables, explaining approximately half the variance in brief-exposure appeal ratings. The result gives a reason to consider composition and colorfulness, while also showing that a universal aesthetic prescription is unjustified. It is not a performance or recruitment study.

Source: [Harvard publication record](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-cc85-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b).

### What portfolio reviewers want to understand

NN/g’s portfolio article reports a survey of 204 UX hiring professionals. It emphasizes relevant projects, individual contributions, decisions, constraints and results, presented so readers can scan. The implication for Nikhil is to expose the research question and important evidence before requiring a long read. The advice concerns UX portfolios, so it is guidance rather than direct evidence about AI hiring.

Source: [5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/).

### Research work needs research artifacts

NN/g’s researcher-specific guidance recommends curating relevant work, presenting artifacts, stating context and contribution, summarizing findings and acknowledging constraints. For this portfolio, evaluation reports, source passages, failure analyses and reproducible repositories are more appropriate evidence than invented client outcomes or decorative dashboards. This is practitioner guidance for UX researchers; the adaptation to AI research is ours.

Source: [Portfolios for UX Researchers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-researcher-portfolio/).

### Grouping and the reported toolkit gap

NN/g’s proximity guidance explains how relative distance communicates relationships, including across responsive layouts. In the rejected draft, the toolkit’s distant second column and excessive space below it weakened its association with the background section. We moved the values next to their label and gave the section a content-sized bottom boundary. The exact pixel values are an implementation choice, not a published scientific optimum.

Source: [Proximity Principle in Visual Design](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/gestalt-proximity/).

### Visual hierarchy and aesthetic impressions

NN/g’s visual hierarchy guidance concerns the order in which size, contrast and grouping direct attention. Its aesthetic-usability article distinguishes perceived ease from actual usability. For this portfolio, a large headline is insufficient if it dominates the real evidence; an attractive preview still needs understandable controls and useful content. These sources do not justify describing a design as productive without observing task performance.

Sources: [Visual Hierarchy in UX](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/), [The Aesthetic-Usability Effect](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-usability-effect/).

### Current practitioner review

Uxcel’s September 2026 article describes a live review of four community portfolios with Réka Nagy. It distinguishes initial relevance, assessment of the work and personal/team context. It also warns that a busy project cover can compete with its headline. This supports concise summaries followed by deeper evidence, with controlled previews. Four reviewed examples and commercial practitioner commentary are not a controlled experiment. An announced forthcoming survey is not treated here as a completed published dataset.

Source: [Uxcel’s original review summary](https://uxcel.com/blog/ux-portfolio-review-red-flags-green-flags).

## First-party design references

[Brittany Chiang](https://brittanychiang.com/) provides a useful structural reference: clear identity, an experience sequence and project material connected to source work. The transferable idea is orientation; copying the widely reused dark layout would not meet the owner’s request for individuality.

[Josh W. Comeau](https://www.joshwcomeau.com/) demonstrates how technical explanations and interactive examples can themselves communicate craft. The transferable idea is useful interaction attached to real content, not importing his visual branding. These are examples, not evidence that their layouts maximize hiring outcomes.

Awwwards collection access was unsuccessful in the research tool, so no award gallery is presented as inspected usability evidence.

## Diagnosis of the current draft

| Problem | Why it matters | Resolution or next requirement |
|---|---|---|
| “01 /” labels and repeated project ordinals | They add a template convention without useful meaning for this reader | Removed from the interface |
| Large toolkit and section gaps | Related content appears detached; more scrolling reveals little new information | Toolkit is now content-sized, with nearby values and smaller section boundaries |
| Nearly identical project-row compositions | The projects appear interchangeable despite different technical questions | Next visual pass needs distinct artifact-led treatments |
| Text-heavy explanations inside colored rectangles | They describe the work without letting the visitor inspect much of it | Use meaningful screenshots, report excerpts or bounded interactive examples |
| Hero dominates the first view | The visitor reaches the substantive work later | Compact identity and position the first selected projects earlier |
| Slogan-like project visuals | They consume the strongest visual area without enough evidence | Replace them with project-specific material |
| Relevant evidence mainly behind details | Readers may miss the most important finding | Put one question, contribution and honest finding in the visible summary |
| Technical checks mistaken for design validation | A page can fit perfectly and still feel bland | Separate layout correctness, visual quality and observed task usability |

## Proposed composition

### Homepage

A compact identity area should name Nikhil, identify his work in retrieval/agents/evaluation, show a smaller portrait and expose CV/contact immediately. A strong selected-work composition should appear early. One dominant project and two supporting projects can establish hierarchy without three identical cards. This asymmetry is an art-direction proposal, not a research-proven winner.

Research outputs should follow, with full titles, venue/date, one clear document action each and a brief connection to the research direction. Product work should remain distinguishable from research and retain prototype status where appropriate. Background and contact should be compact and easy to find.

### Case-study content

Each selected project should have a directly linkable reading surface with the same information structure: question, contribution, design decision, inspectable artifact, validation, limitation and source. Layout can vary around that shared structure. A separate case page is a proposal for the next pass; current project details are still inline disclosures.

| Project | Proposed centerpiece | What must remain explicit |
|---|---|---|
| DocAtlas | Retrieval workspace or source-inspection example, with an actual failure note | Valid quotations do not guarantee a supported answer |
| ModelGuard | Inspectable baseline/candidate/recovery comparison using its real recorded demo artifact | The data is synthetic and does not represent live model quality |
| DocMend | Actual signature-change evidence, proposed patch and guarded-apply conditions | The example is constructed; live and human-reviewed gates remain pending |

### Interaction and art direction

Use structured contrast, stronger composition and project material to add depth. Permit small hover/focus responses and an explicit user-controlled example where it genuinely teaches something. Avoid scroll locking, hover-only information, modal-only project access, decorative counters, arrow glyphs and cinematic background layers. Do not animate content into different reading positions while someone is inspecting it.

Color and type should form a consistent identity, but no source reviewed establishes an optimal font pair or a productivity-enhancing hue for this particular portfolio. Those choices need visual judgment and audience feedback.

## Validation plan for the richer design

The current draft has automated and browser layout checks documented in the separate audit. A richer revision must repeat them rather than inheriting the old pass result.

To evaluate actual usefulness, give unfamiliar readers tasks: identify the owner’s focus, choose a relevant project, explain his contribution, identify a reported limitation, open supporting evidence and find contact/CV. Record completion, confusion and unnecessary actions. Time-to-find and comprehension are useful observations; any target times must be labeled as our acceptance goals, not universal facts from these studies.

The owner’s current assessment is that the design is too simple. That feedback remains unresolved by the existing technical passes. The next pass should be evaluated for both visual character and task clarity before deployment.
